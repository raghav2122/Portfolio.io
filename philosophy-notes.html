Module 1: Nature of Philosophy & Traditional Divisions

1.1 What is Philosophy?

Philosophy is a critical and reflective inquiry into the fundamental laws and principles of everything. It seeks to know the essential nature of things — the Truth or Reality.

The search for the stable and permanent as opposed to perpetual flux.

A philosopher asks: Is reality only matter in continuous motion? Or is it also a mental process? Is it blind or purposive?

Key Defining Features

1. Goes beyond common sense. Philosophy does not confine itself to ordinary, pre-reflective beliefs. Like science, it appeals to reason — but goes deeper. We all know what reality is in a physical sense. But philosophy asks — what IS reality at its core?

2. Searches for definitions and universals. When we examine what art is, the question isn’t about knowing various divisions of art, but defining what art IS. An intellectual inquiry beyond multiplicities and diversities into essences or universals.

3. Normative character. In searching for what IS the case, philosophy moves into what SHOULD be the case. In defining man as a rational being, philosophy suggests how humans ought to be. In defining what education is, we want to see this ideal in our actual institutions.

Substance question: Is reality physical, spiritual, or both?

Monism — one substance • Dualism — two substances • Pluralism — many substances

1.2 Traditional Divisions of Philosophy

Metaphysics: Appearance vs Reality — beyond the visible, there is a world of Ideas/Forms. Deals with the structure of reality: Matter, Space, Time.

Epistemology (Theory of Knowledge): Origin and process of knowledge. Criteria and limits of knowledge. Sources of knowledge — which are reliable, Senses or Reason? Faulty knowledge and theories of error.

Logic: Science of correct reasoning. Basis of all inquiries.

Ethics: Questions of good/bad, right/wrong. Not about prescriptions but knowing what “good” is. Three approaches: Deontological, Utilitarian, Virtue Ethics. Questions of justice and moral dilemma.

Philosophy of Religion: Existence of God and the proofs — Ontological (God’s existence follows from the concept), Causal (everything has a cause → First Cause), Teleological (design implies designer). Also pantheism (Nature is God), the Absolute, and immortality of the soul.

Aesthetics: Philosophy of art. Appreciation of beauty in natural and artistic creations. Does art have a validity of its own?

Module 2: Greek Philosophy

2.1 Pre-Socratic Thinkers

The earliest Greek philosophers asked: What is the basic substance (arche) of everything?

Thales — Water is the fundamental substance. First to seek a natural (not mythological) explanation.

Anaximander — The basic substance is the Apeiron (“Boundless/Indefinite”). Infinite, eternal, indeterminate. More abstract than Thales.

Anaximenes — Air is fundamental. Rarefaction produces fire; condensation produces wind, clouds, water, earth, stone.

Heraclitus — Fire is the element. “Everything flows” (panta rhei). “You cannot step into the same river twice.” BUT there is Logos (reason/law) governing all change. Opposites are necessary and unified. Conflict is the father of all things.

Parmenides — Opposed Heraclitus. Being IS. Non-being is NOT. Change is impossible and illusory. Reality is one, unchanging, eternal, indivisible. Senses deceive; only rational thought grasps truth.

Democritus — Atomism: Reality = atoms (indivisible particles) + void (empty space). All change = atoms rearranging. Purely mechanical, no purpose. Anticipates modern materialism.

Pythagoras — Numbers are the essence of all things. Mathematical relationships underlie reality. Soul is immortal (transmigration).

2.1 (cont.) Socrates

Shifted focus from cosmology to ethics and human life — “Know thyself.”

The Socratic Method (Dialectic/Elenchus): Conversational questioning to expose contradictions and reach universal definitions. Socrates claimed to know nothing (“I know that I know nothing”) — he was a midwife helping others give birth to knowledge.

  • Knowledge = Virtue. If you truly know what is good, you will do it. Wrongdoing comes from ignorance.
  • Sought universal definitions: What IS justice? Courage? Piety?
  • “The unexamined life is not worth living.”
  • Executed (hemlock) for “corrupting the youth” and “impiety” — chose death over abandoning philosophy.

2.2 Plato on Knowledge and Ideas/Forms

The Theory of Ideas (Forms)

Plato divides reality into two worlds:

  1. The World of Ideas/Forms — eternal, unchanging, perfect, immaterial. The TRUE reality. Known only through reason.
  2. The World of Appearances — physical, changing, imperfect copies. Known through sensation (unreliable).

Physical objects “participate” in or are “copies” of their Forms. The Form of the Good is the highest — it illuminates all others, like the sun illuminates the physical world.

The Allegory of the Cave

Prisoners chained in a cave, facing a wall. Fire behind them casts shadows on the wall — they think shadows ARE reality. One prisoner is freed, sees real objects, then the sun (Form of the Good). Returns to tell others but they think he’s crazy.

Meaning: Cave = world of senses. Shadows = appearances. Sun = Form of the Good. Freed prisoner = the philosopher. Philosophy = journey from darkness (ignorance) to light (knowledge).

The Divided Line (Epistemology)

DOXA (Opinion) — about the physical world:

  1. Eikasia — imagination, illusions, shadows (lowest)
  2. Pistis — belief about physical objects

EPISTEME (True Knowledge) — about the Forms:

  1. Dianoia — mathematical/rational thinking, uses hypotheses
  2. Noesis — direct apprehension of Forms through dialectic (highest)

Knowledge as Reminiscence (Anamnesis)

Before entering the body, the soul existed in the world of Forms and knew them directly. Upon entering, it forgets. Sensations don’t produce knowledge — they trigger remembering.

All knowledge is reminiscence. All learning is reawakening.

This proves the prior existence and immortality of the soul.

The union with the body is a hindrance to the soul’s intellectual aspirations. The soul is rational, but when it enters the body, a mortal, irrational part is added.

2.3 Plato on Self, Virtues and Society

Three Parts of the Soul

  1. Rational Part (Reason) — intellectual faculty, capable of knowing the highest good. Includes gentleness, humility, reverence. Must rule.
  2. Spirited Part (Will/Spirit) — executive faculty. Anger, ambition, resentment. Ally of reason against appetites.
  3. Appetitive Part (Desire) — desires for wealth, pleasure, bodily satisfactions. Must be controlled.

The Four Cardinal Virtues

VirtueWhen?
WisdomThe rational part rules properly
CourageThe spirited part holds fast to reason’s instructions about what to fear
TemperanceHarmony; appetites are controlled by reason
JusticeEach part doing its own work properly

The ethical ideal is a well-ordered soul. A life of reason/virtue is the highest good. The just man is the happy man.

The State (mirrors the soul)

ClassSoul ElementFunctionVirtue
Philosopher-KingsRationalGovernanceWisdom
Warriors/GuardiansSpiritedDefenseCourage
Artisans/Merchants/FarmersAppetitiveProductionTemperance

Justice in the state = each class doing its own work. The state is an educational institution; its foundation must be in philosophy. If all men were rational and virtuous, there would be no need for the state.

2.4 Aristotle’s Criticisms of Plato + Aristotle’s Metaphysics

Problems with Plato’s Theory of Ideas

1. Unjustified separation of eternal ideas from the actual world of experience.

2. Degrades the physical world as mere “appearance.”

3. “Copies” and “participation” don’t explain anything — saying individual man “participates” in ideal man adds nothing.

4. Third Man Argument: If an individual man resembles the Form of Man, we need a THIRD “man” to explain what they share → infinite regress.

5. Forms are static/changeless — can’t explain change and motion. Change requires both being AND becoming.

Substance (Hylomorphism): Form + Matter are Indivisible

You cannot separate the essence of a thing from its material. The idea of gold can’t be understood apart from its properties (yellowish, shiny, malleable). Even raw matter has some form. Aristotle’s position is pluralistic — reality = many individual substances.

Potentiality and Actuality

Matter has potentiality to become something. A seed is a potential tree; the tree is an actualized seed. Each stage is a form realized. Matter and form are relative — an acorn is a potential tree, the tree is an actualized acorn.

Teleology and God

The world is purposive, not mechanical. “Nature does not create anything in vain.”

God = highest form/actuality, no potentiality = Actus Purus (Pure Act) = Prime Mover (Unmoved Mover). Remains outside the world-process, supplies motion by attraction (as an object of love/desire). World is imperfect because matter is incapable of fully achieving its purpose.

The Four Causes

CauseQuestionExample (Making a Pot)
MaterialWhat is it made of?Clay
FormalWhat is it? (design/essence)The concept/design of the pot
EfficientBy what is it made?The potter
FinalFor what purpose?To store/cool water

All four operate simultaneously. The final cause (purpose) is the most important — it explains WHY something exists.

Module 3: Continental Rationalism

3.1 What is Rationalism?

Rationalism holds that reason (not sense experience) is the primary source of knowledge. The model is mathematics — starting from self-evident axioms and deducing necessary truths with certainty. Key figures: Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz.

3.2–3.4 René Descartes (1596–1650)

The Method of Doubt

Descartes wants to find ONE absolutely certain truth. His method: doubt everything that can possibly be doubted.

  1. Doubt the senses: Senses sometimes deceive (optical illusions). If they deceive sometimes, maybe always.
  2. The dream argument: Dreams feel real. Can never be certain you’re not dreaming right now.
  3. The evil demon (malin génie): Perhaps an all-powerful evil demon is deceiving you about everything — even mathematics.

“Cogito ergo sum” — “I think, therefore I am.”

Even if the demon deceives me about everything, he cannot deceive me about the fact that I’m being deceived. If I doubt, I think. If I think, I exist. The self as a thinking thing is the one indubitable truth.

Criterion of knowledge: Whatever is perceived clearly and distinctly must be true.

Substance, Attributes, and Modes

SubstanceAttribute (Essential Property)Modes (Variations)
GodSubstance in the absolute sense—
Mind (Res Cogitans)ThoughtImagination, will, perception
Body (Res Extensa)ExtensionFigure, motion

Mind and body are distinct — their attributes are completely different. Body is extended but passive; mind is active and free. Mind has no extension; no body can think. We CAN conceive extension without figure, and thought without imagination — so extension and thought are the essential attributes.

Mind-Body Dualism

Nature is left free for mechanical explanations. The human body is a machine. Heat in heart is the moving principle; muscles are organs of motion; nerves are organs of sensation.

The Interaction Problem: If mind and body exclude each other completely, how can they interact? Mind cannot cause change in body; body cannot cause change in mind. Yet hunger, thirst, emotions, pain all involve BOTH.

“If I were just a thinking being without a body, I would probably know that I am hungry but not feel that I am hungry.”

Descartes’ Solution: The Pineal Gland

Movements in “animal spirits” (fine particles in blood) are caused by objects → transferred to the pineal gland → sensations. Soul can also move the gland → motion to animal spirits → through nerves into muscles → bodily movement.

But this fails: He never explains HOW an unextended mind pushes a physical gland. The same dualism problem reappears at the pineal gland.

Spinoza (1632–1677)

Critique of Descartes

“Dependent substance” is a contradiction. If substance exists in itself, then mind and body CANNOT be substances — they depend on God.

Spinoza’s Monism: God is the ONLY substance. Thought and extension are attributes of the one God. God = Nature (Deus sive Natura). This is pantheism.

Dualism of substance disappears. Dualism of attributes remains.

Parallelism (vs Interaction)

Since thought and extension are attributes of ONE substance, there is no interaction between them. They run in parallel. Every mental event has a corresponding physical event, and vice versa. They correspond but do not cause each other — like two clocks synchronized but not connected.

Descartes vs Spinoza

Module 4: British Empiricism

4.1 Introduction + John Locke (1632–1704)

Empiricism: All knowledge comes from experience, not innate reason. The senses are primary. Key figures: Locke, Berkeley, Hume — each more radical than the last.

Rationalism (Intuitionism / Apriorism)Empiricism (Sensationalism)
Reason as the source and norm of knowledgeExperience as the source
Genuine knowledge has its foundation in reasonNo inborn truths; all knowledge from sense experience
Certain truths are natural, inborn, a priori and validNecessary propositions yield only probable knowledge

Nature, Origin & Validity of Knowledge

For Locke, philosophy is the true knowledge of things including physics (nature of things), ethics, and semiotics/logic. We must examine the source of knowledge to talk about its certainty and limits.

Refutation of Innate Ideas

Locke’s first move is to demolish the rationalist doctrine that certain ideas are innate (inborn in the mind):

1. It is a near contradiction that truths are imprinted on the soul yet the mind is unconscious of them — nothing can be in mind of which it is not conscious.

2. If propositions (like identity, impossibility) are innate, they should be immediately perceived by everyone, even infants and idiots. They are not.

3. To say propositions are “dispositions” (capable of being perceived under certain circumstances) does not prove their primitiveness, if the mind alone is capable of knowing all truths.

4. Moral laws are not innate — what is sin to some may be a duty to others.

5. The idea of God cannot be innate — certain tribes may lack or have no clear impression of him. Universal ideas like fire, sun, heat do not establish the innateness of God.

Mind as Tabula Rasa (Blank Slate)

The mind is a “dark chamber,” a “white paper” void of all characters and ideas. All materials of reason and knowledge are furnished by experience.

Two sources of experience:

  1. Sensation — supplies sensible qualities from external objects
  2. Reflection (internal sense) — supplies ideas of the mind’s own operations: perception, thinking, doubting, willing

The primary ability of the mind is its intellectual ability to receive impressions from outside or from its own operations. An idea is an immediate object of perception.

Simple Ideas

The mind has the power to repeat, compare, and combine simple ideas to make complex ones. But the mind has no power to invent simple ideas — they must come from experience.

  • Simple ideas can have one attribute: sky is blue, lemons are sour
  • Some come from multiple senses: extension, figure, motion, rest enter through both sight and touch
  • Some from reflection only: discerning, naming, compounding
  • Some from both sensation and reflection: pleasure, pain, existence, succession, duration

Complex Ideas

The mind creates complex ideas by compounding, comparing, and abstraction. Mind is passive when receiving simple ideas but exerts power over them to make complex ideas. Three types:

1. Modes — dependencies on substances (e.g. triangle, gratitude, murder). A simple mode is the variation of the same simple idea in different combinations (e.g. a dozen, a score). Mixed modes are compounded of simple ideas of several kinds (e.g. beauty = composition of color and figure causing delight).

2. Substance — a combination of ideas of qualities, supposed to represent a distinct particular thing, and a confused idea of a “support” or bearer of these qualities. E.g. “gold” = bearer of whitish color, weight, hardness, fusibility, etc. We cannot imagine qualities existing by themselves, so we suppose a substratum in which they subsist. We have ideas of both material and spiritual substances.

3. Relations — by comparing, the mind discerns relations between things. Cause and effect is the most comprehensive relation. Other relations: time, place, identity, difference.

Primary and Secondary Qualities

The power that objects have to produce definite ideas in us is called qualities. Many of our ideas resemble external qualities (exact images).

Primary (Original) QualitiesSecondary Qualities
Inherent, inseparable from the objectNothing in objects themselves — just powers
Solidity, Figure, Extension, Motion/Rest, NumberColors, Sounds, Tastes, Smells, Temperature
Our ideas RESEMBLE the qualitiesProduced in us by the primary qualities; do NOT resemble anything in the object

Validity and Evidence of Knowledge

Ideas should be clear and distinct. Real ideas correspond to real existent things (their archetypes). Simple ideas are all real as effects of powers outside our minds. Mixed modes and relations have no reality outside the mind. Ideas of substances are intended as representations of external substances.

Knowledge is the perception of the agreement or disagreement (repugnancy) of our ideas. Three degrees of evidence:

1. Intuitive: The mind perceives agreement/disagreement by direct inspection, without any other idea. That a circle is not a triangle, that three is greater than two. Clear, self-evident, most certain.

2. Demonstrative: Not perceived immediately, but established by comparing with other ideas (proofs). Certain, yet its evidence is not as clear as intuitive.

3. Sensitive: We have ideas of external objects in mind. Can we infer their reality? Our ordinary perceptions are beyond reasonable doubt, but they do not reach intuitive or demonstrative certainty.

In both intuitive and demonstrative knowledge, we have certainty. Sensitive knowledge falls short.

Limits of Knowledge

Since knowledge is perception of agreement/disagreement of ideas, it cannot reach further than ideas. Where ideas are wanting, there can be no knowledge.

  • We do not experience everything we are capable of
  • We do not understand everything we perceive
  • We do not see what connection there is between primary and secondary qualities
  • From the idea of gold as a yellow metal, we cannot infer that all gold is malleable
  • Only knowledge of universal self-evident truths truly satisfies us

Locke can form abstract ideas by separating common features from particular instances — this is what Berkeley will attack.

4.2 Berkeley — Rejection of Abstract Ideas

The Attack on Locke

If all we know is our ideas (as Locke says), we have no basis to claim an external material world exists independent of perception.

Rejection of Abstract Ideas

The triangle example: A general idea of “triangle” that is “neither oblique nor rectangular, equilateral, nor scalenon, but all and none of these at once” — nothing corresponds to this in our thoughts. Logical inconsistency.

The motion example: We cannot frame the idea of motion distinct from a body moving, which is neither swift nor slow, neither curvilinear nor rectilinear.

We cannot see or feel anything without an actual sensation. We cannot conceive any sensible thing distinct from its perception. Even imagination is of concrete qualities.

Esse est Percipi — “To be is to be perceived”

“When I say the table I write on exists, I mean that I see and feel it. When I am outside the room, I mean if I were inside I might perceive it, or some other mind does actually perceive it.”

Sensations exist only in the mind. Their existence IS their being perceived.

Against Primary/Secondary Distinction

Even “primary” qualities (solidity, extension) come from touch and sight — they’re sensations in my mind. I can never perceive extension without color. I cannot abstract secondary qualities away and leave “solid extended substance.” All qualities are secondary. Bodies have no existence without the mind.

Berkeley is a subjective idealist, not a skeptic. Things are real as ideas in minds. God perceives everything at all times.

4.3–4.4 David Hume (1711–1776)

Impressions and Ideas

Impressions: Vivid, lively perceptions — sensations, emotions as they first appear in the soul.

Ideas: Faint copies of impressions — what we recall or reflect on.

All knowledge = compounding, transposing, diminishing impressions. If you can’t trace an idea to an impression, it’s meaningless.

Association of Ideas

  1. Resemblance — picture leads to original
  2. Contiguity — mention of one room suggests adjacent one
  3. Cause and Effect — wound calls up idea of pain

Two Kinds of Knowledge

Relations of IdeasMatters of Fact
Math, geometry, logicEverything else
Certain, discoverable by thought aloneBased on experience, only probable
Denial involves contradictionDenial involves NO contradiction
“3 × 5 = 15”“The sun will rise tomorrow”

Denial of Causality

All evidence of matters of fact rests on cause and effect. But is there a necessary connection between cause and effect?

We do NOT reach causal knowledge a priori: No reasoning alone can discover the explosiveness of gunpowder. The effect is totally different from the cause. Reason alone can’t tell us bread nourishes or fire warms.

No necessary connection: There is no logical contradiction in “fire doesn’t warm” or “bread doesn’t nourish.” Unlike “a triangle has 4 sides.”

What we actually observe: Objects succeed one another. Heat follows flame. One billiard ball’s motion is “attended by” another’s. We NEVER perceive any “power,” “force,” or necessary connection. It’s entirely concealed.

Hume’s answer: Constant conjunction + habit/custom → belief in connection. The mind, having seen the same sequence many times, EXPECTS it to continue. The “necessary connection” is just a feeling in our minds from repeated experience, not something in objects.

Even our own will: The connection between willing and bodily movement is equally mysterious. We experience the sequence (willing → movement) but never perceive the mechanism or power.

Hume’s Skepticism

Metaphysics, theology, and natural sciences cannot give universal/necessary knowledge. We only know experiences and reach only probability. Ultimates, substances, causes, soul, external world — we know nothing of them with certainty.

Progression of Empiricism:
Locke: ideas + external world + God
Berkeley: ideas + God (drops external world)
Hume: only impressions and ideas (drops God and substance too)
Module 5: Ethics — Hume, Kant, Bentham, Mill

5.1 Nature and Scope of Ethics + Hume’s Ethics

Ethics = philosophical study of morality. What makes actions right or wrong? What is “good”? Not about listing rules but understanding foundations of moral judgment.

Hume on Ethics

Moral sentiments — morality is based on feeling, not reason. Reason is and ought to be the “slave of the passions.” We approve actions that produce pleasure/utility through a moral SENSE. Sympathy shapes moral judgments.

The Is-Ought Problem (Hume’s Guillotine): You cannot derive an “ought” from an “is.” Factual statements cannot logically produce value judgments. Just because something IS a certain way doesn’t mean it SHOULD be.

5.2 Kant on Duty (Deontological Ethics)

For Kant, morality is grounded in REASON, not feeling. The opposite of Hume.

The Good Will

The only thing good without qualification is a good will — the intention to do right BECAUSE it is right. Intelligence, courage, wealth can all be used for evil. Even if a good will accomplishes nothing, it still “shines like a jewel.” Consequences are irrelevant to moral worth.

Duty vs Inclination

An action has moral worth ONLY when done from duty, not from inclination or self-interest.

  • Shopkeeper who is honest to keep customers → self-interest, no moral worth
  • Shopkeeper who is honest because honesty is right → from duty, morally worthy
  • Helping because you enjoy it → inclination, no moral worth
  • Helping purely because it’s your duty → morally worthy

The Categorical Imperative (3 Formulations)

1. Universal Law: “Act only according to that maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law.”

Test: What if EVERYONE did this? Lying → if all lied, trust collapses, lying becomes impossible. Self-defeating → wrong.

2. Humanity as End: “Treat humanity, whether in your own person or that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means.”

Every rational being has intrinsic worth (dignity). Slavery, deception, exploitation all violate this.

3. Kingdom of Ends: Act as if legislating for a community where everyone is both ruler and subject.

Criticisms of Kant

1. Too rigid: Must tell truth even to a murderer asking where your friend is.

2. Ignores consequences: Good-intention action with terrible outcomes is still “morally worthy.”

3. Conflicting duties: Truth-telling vs protecting the innocent — no resolution method.

4. Multiple maxims: “I will lie” vs “I will lie to save a life” give different results.

5. Empty formalism: Tells HOW to test maxims but gives no specific content.

6. Devalues compassion: Helping from genuine care has no moral worth.

5.3–5.4 Bentham and Mill’s Utilitarianism

Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832)

Principle of Utility (Greatest Happiness Principle): An action is right if it produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. Happiness = pleasure + absence of pain.

Psychological hedonism: Humans naturally seek pleasure and avoid pain (descriptive).
Ethical hedonism: Humans OUGHT to maximize pleasure (normative).

The Hedonistic Calculus (Felicific Calculus) — 7 Criteria

  1. Intensity — How strong?
  2. Duration — How long?
  3. Certainty — How probable?
  4. Propinquity — How soon?
  5. Fecundity — Will it lead to more pleasure?
  6. Purity — Is it free from pain?
  7. Extent — How many people affected?

All pleasures are equal in kind — only QUANTITY matters. “Pushpin is as good as poetry” if it gives equal pleasure.

Criticisms of Bentham:

1. “Pig philosophy” — reduces ethics to animal satisfaction.

2. Calculus is impractical — can’t measure pleasure in numbers.

3. Tyranny of majority — could justify torturing one if millions enjoy it.

4. Ignores justice and motives entirely.

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873)

Higher and Lower Pleasures: Pleasures differ in QUALITY, not just quantity.

“It is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.”

Intellectual pleasures (philosophy, literature, art) are qualitatively SUPERIOR to bodily pleasures (eating, physical comfort).

Competent Judges: Who decides which pleasures are higher? Those who have experienced BOTH kinds. They would unanimously prefer intellectual pleasures.

Rule Utilitarianism: Follow RULES that generally maximize happiness (don’t recalculate every time). “Don’t lie” is good because lying generally causes more unhappiness.

Harm Principle: Liberty can only be restricted to prevent harm to others — never for your own good.

Justice = highest utility. Rules of justice are the rules whose observance produces the most long-term happiness.

Module 6: Existentialism

6.1 Existentialism vs Essentialism

What is Existentialism?

A philosophy against extreme rationalism because it gives prominence to man’s passionate nature — his anguish, guilt, sense of freedom.

  • A form of “faith philosophy” — asserts human freedom, a form of individualism recognizing the importance of decisions in relation to others (not committed to a theory of free will)
  • Existence precedes essence. There is no essential nature of humans. Refusal to identify being with consciousness, spirit, mind, or thought. Rejection of Idealism.
  • The individual as a whole is the subject of concern. Choice, decision, anxiety, dread, freedom, nothingness are the central issues.
  • Views human life as a series of critical situations and resolutions.

“Existence precedes essence” (Sartre) — You exist first, then define yourself through choices. There is no pre-given “human nature.”

Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855) — Father of Existentialism

An early 19th century Danish philosopher who gave the term “Existenz” and influenced German and French existentialism. Trained in theology, developed a philosophy on the Christian conception of man as an individual aware of himself in his choices and commitments.

Predominantly religious and theological, but equally concerned with man’s ethical nature. His ethical philosophy is individualistic: each individual is confronted with ethical choices which he alone can make and for which he assumes sole responsibility. Every decision is irrevocable and presents a necessity for subsequent decision.

Three Principal Conceptions: Truth, Choice, and God

Truth: His notion is Socratic — “truth is not introduced into the individual from without but within him all the time.” Truth is not about abstract thinking but the actual, concrete individual. Existentialist truth is a passionate, inner commitment to something not objectively certain — it is equivalent to faith. Truth is subjectivity.

Choice: Expounded in Either/Or. No psychological description of choice is possible — it must be experienced. Choice is a decision between two ways of life: (a) the esthetic life, devoted to art, music, drama; and (b) the ethical life, which seeks happiness in marriage, business, or a profession.

God: The culmination of existentialist thinking is the third stage — knowledge of God. An individual establishes rapport with him, preserves his individuality, and God remains an “Absolute Other.”

Three Stages of Existence

StageMode of LivingModelOutcome
AestheticLiving for pleasure, sensation, novelty, the momentDon JuanBoredom, emptiness, DESPAIR
EthicalDuty, moral commitment, responsibility, consistencyThe married personCan become rigid; can’t address deepest questions
ReligiousPersonal, passionate relationship with God; beyond ethicsAbraham (sacrifice of Isaac)“Teleological suspension of the ethical”

Each transition requires a leap of faith — not rational progression but a decisive, passionate choice. The one who makes this leap is the “knight of faith.”

Key Concepts

Boredom, Anxiety (Angst), and Despair: Not fear of something specific but deep unease accompanying freedom. We are anxious BECAUSE we are free. “The dizziness of freedom.” Despair = failure to be one’s authentic self (“the sickness unto death”).

“What is important is not what we must know but what we must do.” No reasoning can help us — every premise presupposes another. The only thing left is choice. And the choices are irrevocable.

Subjectivity is Truth: Truth must be lived and personally appropriated. Not WHAT you believe but HOW — with passion and commitment.

The crowd is untruth: The public dulls individual existence. People hide behind collective opinions to avoid making their own choices.

Martin Heidegger (1889–1976)

Central question: What is the meaning of Being (Sein)?

Dasein (Da = there, Sein = being) = “being-there” or being-in-the-world — human existence. We are the beings who ASK about Being. Influenced by Franz Brentano’s concept of intentionality. Overcomes the subject-object dichotomy.

Dasein ist sorge — Dasein IS Care (Sorge). Concern for human finitude, our consumed existence.

Two modes of encountering things:

  • Zuhandenheit (readiness-to-hand) — things as we USE them, engaged, practical (hammer as a tool)
  • Vorhandenheit (present-at-hand) — things as we OBSERVE them, detached, theoretical (hammer as an object of study)

Thrownness-Projection-Fallenness:

  • Thrownness (Geworfenheit) — we didn’t choose to be born, our culture, era, or family. We are “thrown” into existence.
  • Projection — we project ourselves into possibilities, always ahead of ourselves.
  • Fallenness — absorption in the everyday world of Das Man (the “They”).

Transcendence with reference to the world, other human beings (mit-sein — being-with-others), and anxiety or care for the future.

Authenticity vs Inauthenticity

Inauthentic: Das Man / Anonymous (the “They”) — Living as “one” does. Doing what people do, thinking what people think. Conformity, surrendering to public opinion. This is the DEFAULT mode.

Authentic: Owning your existence. Making decisions based on your own understanding. Confronting mortality.

Being-toward-Death

Death is our ownmost (no one can die for me), non-relational (isolates me from all), certain (I will die), and indefinite (I don’t know when) possibility.

In everyday life we cover this up: “One dies” — but not me, not now. Authentically facing mortality frees us from the tyranny of Das Man.

Possibilities and Angst (Anxiety): Has no specific object. Reveals the nothingness underlying existence. Strips away everyday familiarity. Gateway to authenticity.

Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980)

Being and Nothingness (1943). Atheistic existentialism. God is a contradictory idea. No predetermined human nature, no cosmic purpose.

Existence Precedes Essence

A paper knife has essence before existence (designed before made). Humans are the reverse: we exist first, then create our essence through choices. No God to conceive “human nature” in advance.

“Man is nothing else but what he makes of himself.” The nature of man is an undetermined project — a form of intentionality to be filled out with content.

Radical Freedom

“Men are condemned to be free.” We didn’t choose to exist, but we are absolutely free and absolutely responsible for everything we do. Freedom — the nature of man is an undetermined project. No excuses — no determinism, no “human nature,” no God’s plan. Even NOT choosing is a choice.

Intentionality

Consciousness is always consciousness OF something. And in being conscious of something, I am aware of my own consciousness. This is a key phenomenological insight Sartre inherits from Husserl and Brentano.

Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi)

Pretending you are not free. Denying your freedom. Deceiving yourself.

  • “I had no choice” — you always have a choice
  • “That’s just who I am” — you are always becoming
  • “I was just following orders” — you chose to follow
  • The waiter who plays at being a waiter too perfectly — performing a role as if it were fixed nature

Bad faith is the fundamental ethical failure.

Anguish, Forlornness, Despair

Anguish: Realizing you are totally free and responsible — not just for yourself, but your choices set an example for all humanity.

Forlornness: God doesn’t exist → no predetermined moral order, no objective values, no cosmic purpose. We are alone, without excuses. We must create our own values.

Despair: We must act without hope of certainty. We can only control our own choices.

Being-in-itself vs Being-for-itself

Simply IS. No freedom, no possibility.Always aware, free, becoming.

6.4 The Ethical Self in Existentialism

No pre-given moral rules. Unlike Kant or utilitarians, existentialism provides no ready-made ethical system.

Choice creates values. Values don’t exist before choices — our choices CREATE values. No external moral authority.

Total responsibility: “Man is condemned to be free... we are left alone, without excuse.” When you choose for yourself, you choose for all humanity.

You cannot blame God, nature, society, upbringing, or emotions.

Authenticity = the highest ethical value. Own your freedom. Make genuine choices. Accept responsibility. Face anxiety and mortality. Create your own meaning.

Bad faith = the fundamental moral failure. Refusing to be free, denying what you truly are.

Ethics of engagement and commitment: Not about detachment but ACTION. You must commit, choose, act. “Not to choose is, in fact, to choose not to choose.”

Q1. Critically discuss Plato’s views on Ideas/Forms and nature of the state. (15 marks)

Theory of Forms: Plato divides reality into two worlds. The World of Ideas/Forms is eternal, unchanging, perfect, and immaterial — this is TRUE reality, known through reason. The World of Appearances is physical, changing, and imperfect — mere copies/shadows of the Forms, known through sensation.

Physical objects “participate” in their respective Forms. A beautiful painting is beautiful because it participates in the Form of Beauty. The Form of the Good is the highest Form — it illuminates all others, like the sun illuminates the physical world.

Allegory of the Cave: Prisoners chained in a cave see only shadows on the wall and take them for reality. A freed prisoner escapes, sees the real world and the sun (Form of the Good), and returns to enlighten others. The cave = world of senses; shadows = appearances; sun = Form of the Good; freed prisoner = the philosopher.

Divided Line: Doxa (opinion) includes eikasia (imagination) and pistis (belief). Episteme (true knowledge) includes dianoia (mathematical reasoning) and noesis (direct apprehension of Forms through dialectic).

Knowledge as Reminiscence: The soul existed before birth in the world of Forms. Upon entering the body, it forgets. Sensations trigger remembering. All knowledge is reminiscence, all learning is reawakening. This proves the soul’s prior existence and immortality.

Three parts of the soul: Rational (wisdom, must rule), Spirited (courage, ally of reason), Appetitive (desires, must be controlled). The four virtues: Wisdom, Courage, Temperance, Justice. Justice = each part doing its own work.

The State mirrors the soul: Philosopher-Kings (rational/wisdom) rule; Warriors (spirited/courage) defend; Artisans/Merchants (appetitive/temperance) produce. Justice in the state = each class doing its own function. The state is an educational institution; its foundation must be in philosophy. If all men were rational, there would be no need for the state — it exists due to imperfections of human nature.

Critical evaluation: Plato’s theory is foundational but faces serious objections. Aristotle’s Third Man Argument shows that “participation” leads to infinite regress. The separation of Forms from the physical world is unjustified. Static Forms cannot explain change and motion. His ideal state is authoritarian — most people are permanently excluded from ruling. Despite this, the theory of Forms remains one of the most influential ideas in Western philosophy.

Q2. Discuss Aristotle’s form-matter and actuality-potentiality relations. Examine his objections against Plato. (7–15 marks)

Objections against Plato: (1) Unjustified separation of Forms from the actual world. (2) Degrades the physical world as mere “appearance.” (3) “Copies” and “participation” are unsatisfactory — saying individual man participates in ideal man adds nothing. (4) The Third Man Argument: if individual man resembles Form of Man, we need a third “man” to explain what they share → infinite regress. (5) Forms are static — cannot explain change and motion.

Form and Matter (Hylomorphism): For Aristotle, form and matter are indivisible. You cannot separate the essence of a thing from its material. The idea of gold cannot be understood apart from its properties (yellowish, shiny, malleable). Even raw matter has some form. His position is pluralistic — reality consists of many individual substances between indeterminate matter (bottom) and God/pure form (top).

Potentiality and Actuality: Matter has potentiality to become something. A seed is a potential tree; the tree is an actualized seed. Each stage is a form realized. Matter and form are relative — an acorn is a potential tree, the tree is an actualized acorn. This elegantly explains change without Plato’s problems.

The Four Causes: Material (what it’s made of — clay), Formal (design/definition — concept of pot), Efficient (agent — the potter), Final (purpose — to store water). All four operate simultaneously. The final cause is most important.

Teleology and God: The world is purposive, not mechanical. God is the highest form/actuality with no potentiality — Actus Purus, the Prime Mover (Unmoved Mover). God remains outside the world-process, supplies motion by attraction. The world is imperfect because matter is incapable of fully achieving its purpose.

Q3. Discuss Descartes’ views on the method and criterion of knowledge. (15 marks)

Method of Doubt: Descartes seeks one absolutely certain truth by doubting everything. (1) Senses sometimes deceive → doubt sensory knowledge. (2) Dreams feel real → can’t be certain we’re not dreaming. (3) Evil demon (malin génie) might deceive about everything, even math.

The Cogito: Even if the demon deceives me, he cannot deceive me about the fact that I am being deceived. If I doubt, I think. If I think, I exist. “Cogito ergo sum” — the one indubitable truth. I exist as a thinking thing (res cogitans).

Criterion: Whatever is perceived clearly and distinctly must be true. The Cogito serves as the model.

Substance, Attributes, Modes: God is substance in the absolute sense. Mind (res cogitans) has the attribute of Thought; its modes are imagination, will, perception. Body (res extensa) has the attribute of Extension; its modes are figure and motion. We can conceive extension without figure, thought without imagination — so these are the essential attributes. Mind and body are distinct because their attributes are distinct. Body is extended but passive; mind is active and free.

Mind-Body Dualism: Body is a machine. But if two substances exclude each other, how can they interact? Yet hunger, emotions, pain all involve both. Descartes’ solution: interaction through the pineal gland. Animal spirits transfer to the gland producing sensations; the soul moves the gland producing bodily movement. But he fails to explain how an unextended mind pushes a physical gland — the same dualism problem reappears.

Q4. Critically examine Descartes and Spinoza on mind-body dualism. (7–15 marks)

Descartes: Three substances — God, Mind (thinking/unextended), Body (extended/passive). Mind and body interact but their natures are completely different. The interaction problem: if they exclude each other, how can they interact? Descartes locates interaction in the pineal gland — but fails to explain how an unextended mind can move a physical gland. His position: the unity of mind and body is “of composition, not of nature.” God put them together but they remain separate.

Spinoza’s critique: “Dependent substance” is a contradiction. If substance is that which exists in itself, mind and body cannot be substances — they depend on God.

Spinoza’s solution: God (or Nature) is the ONLY substance (Deus sive Natura = pantheism). Thought and extension are not separate substances but attributes of one God. Dualism of substance disappears; dualism of attributes remains. Mind and body don’t interact — they run in parallel. Every mental event has a corresponding physical event. They are like two clocks synchronized but not causally connected.

Critical evaluation: Descartes preserves our intuition that mind and body interact (we feel this daily) but can’t explain the mechanism. Spinoza avoids the interaction problem entirely but at the cost of denying interaction — which seems to contradict everyday experience. Descartes gives us substance dualism; Spinoza gives us attribute dualism within monism. Neither fully resolves the mind-body problem, which remains one of philosophy’s hardest questions.

Q5. Discuss Berkeley’s rejection of abstract ideas and Hume’s rejection of certainty in factual matters. (15 marks)

Berkeley’s Rejection of Abstract Ideas: Berkeley attacks Locke. If all we know is our ideas, we cannot establish an independent external world. The claim of an independent world rests on the doctrine that the mind can frame abstract ideas — separating qualities from perceived objects.

Berkeley says this is impossible. The general idea of a “triangle” that is “neither oblique nor rectangular, equilateral, nor scalenon, but all and none at once” has nothing corresponding to it in our thoughts — logical inconsistency. We cannot frame the idea of motion apart from a body moving.

We cannot see or feel anything without an actual sensation. Esse est percipi — to be is to be perceived. Sensations exist only in the mind. Even primary qualities (solidity, extension) come from touch and sight — they are sensations in my mind. I can never perceive extension without color. All qualities are secondary. Bodies have no existence without the mind. Berkeley is a subjective idealist, not a skeptic — things are real as ideas in minds; God perceives everything.

Hume’s Rejection of Certainty: All knowledge divides into relations of ideas (math, logic — certain) and matters of fact (everything else — only probable). All evidence of matters of fact rests on cause and effect. But is there a necessary connection?

We do NOT reach causal knowledge a priori. The effect is totally different from the cause. From the first appearance of an object, we can never predict its effect. There is no logical contradiction in “fire doesn’t warm” or “bread doesn’t nourish.”

What we actually observe: objects succeed one another. Heat follows flame, one billiard ball’s motion is attended by another’s. We never perceive any “power” or “force.” Constant conjunction + habit/custom produces the belief in connection. The “necessary connection” is a feeling in our minds from repeated experience, not something in objects. Even our own will moving our body — we don’t perceive the power. Metaphysics and natural sciences cannot give universal/necessary knowledge; only probability.

Q6. Discuss the distinction between primary and secondary qualities and analyze Berkeley’s views on knowledge. (7 marks)

Locke’s distinction: Primary qualities are inherent and inseparable from the object — solidity, figure, extension, motion, number. Our ideas of them resemble the actual qualities. Secondary qualities are not in objects themselves — colors, sounds, tastes, smells are powers to produce sensations in us through their primary qualities. Our ideas do not resemble anything in the object.

Berkeley’s attack: This distinction is wrong. Even primary qualities (solidity, extension) come from touch and sight — they are sensations in my mind. I can never perceive an extended thing without color. I cannot abstract secondary qualities and leave behind an “extended solid substance.” All qualities are equally mind-dependent. Even if such a substance existed outside the mind, how could we know it? All our ideas are inactive.

Berkeley’s view on knowledge: Esse est percipi — to be is to be perceived. Bodies have no existence without the mind. We always begin with sensory qualities (heat, color, smell), either directly perceived (impressions — vivid) or imagined/remembered (ideas — faint copies). Things like tables and chairs are composed of ideas. Berkeley does not deny universals of sensory qualities but they are not abstract ideas. He is a subjective idealist who redefines reality as mental, with God as the eternal perceiver.

Q7. Critically evaluate Kant’s categorical imperatives and Bentham’s Hedonistic Calculus. (15 marks)

Kant’s Categorical Imperative: Morality is grounded in reason, not feeling. The only unconditionally good thing is a good will — the intention to do right because it is right. Actions have moral worth only when done from duty, not inclination.

Three formulations: (1) Universal Law — “Act only according to that maxim by which you can will it to become a universal law.” Lying fails because universal lying destroys trust. (2) Humanity as End — treat humanity always as an end, never merely as a means. (3) Kingdom of Ends — act as if legislating for a community of rational beings.

Criticisms: Too rigid (must tell truth to a murderer). Ignores consequences. Conflicting duties with no resolution. Multiple maxims can describe the same action. Empty formalism. Devalues compassion.

Bentham’s Hedonistic Calculus: The Principle of Utility — greatest happiness of the greatest number. Happiness = pleasure, unhappiness = pain. Seven criteria to measure pleasure: Intensity, Duration, Certainty, Propinquity (nearness), Fecundity (leads to more pleasure?), Purity (free from pain?), Extent (how many affected). All pleasures are equal in kind — only quantity matters. “Pushpin is as good as poetry.”

Criticisms: “Pig philosophy” — no distinction of quality. Calculus is impractical. Can justify tyranny of majority. Ignores justice and motives.

Comparison: Kant grounds morality in duty/intention; Bentham in consequences/pleasure. Kant says consequences are irrelevant; Bentham says they are everything. Kant treats persons as ends; Bentham can sacrifice one for many. Kant allows no exceptions; Bentham allows exceptions for greater happiness. Both have strengths — Kant protects individual dignity, Bentham considers real-world outcomes — but both face serious objections.

Q8. Compare Bentham and Mill’s utilitarian ethics. (7–9 marks)

Both are utilitarians — both hold that the right action maximizes happiness. But Mill refines Bentham significantly.

Bentham: Only quantitative differences in pleasure. “Pushpin is as good as poetry” if equal quantity. Measures via the felicific calculus (7 criteria). More act utilitarian — judge each individual action. Psychological hedonism (humans naturally seek pleasure) and ethical hedonism (they ought to).

Mill’s refinements: (1) Pleasures differ in QUALITY, not just quantity. Intellectual pleasures (philosophy, art) are qualitatively superior to bodily pleasures. “It is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.” (2) Competent judges (those who’ve experienced both kinds) decide which are higher. (3) More rule utilitarian — follow rules that generally maximize happiness. (4) The Harm Principle — liberty restricted only to prevent harm to others. (5) Justice is the highest utility.

Key contrast: Bentham sees humans as equal to animals in pleasure terms; Mill insists humans have higher faculties. Bentham is crude but democratic; Mill is more nuanced but potentially elitist (who decides “higher”?). Mill’s qualitative distinction arguably contradicts pure utilitarianism — if quality matters independently, it’s no longer just about maximizing pleasure.

Q9. Discuss Locke’s empiricism — refutation of innate ideas, tabula rasa, and validity of knowledge. (7–15 marks)

Refutation of innate ideas: Locke attacks the rationalist doctrine that certain truths are inborn. (1) Contradictory that truths are imprinted on the soul yet the mind is unconscious of them. (2) If innate, propositions should be perceived by everyone, even infants and idiots — they are not. (3) Dispositions don’t prove primitiveness. (4) Moral laws aren’t innate — what is sin to some is duty to others. (5) The idea of God isn’t innate — some tribes lack any impression of him.

Tabula Rasa: The mind is a “dark chamber,” a “white paper” void of all ideas. All knowledge comes from experience via two sources: sensation (external — sensible qualities) and reflection (internal — mind’s own operations like perceiving, thinking, doubting). An idea is an immediate object of perception.

Simple and Complex Ideas: The mind can repeat, compare, and combine simple ideas into complex ones, but cannot invent simple ideas. Complex ideas are of three types: Modes (dependencies on substances, e.g. triangle, beauty), Substance (combination of qualities + confused idea of a “substratum” or bearer), Relations (cause-effect is the most comprehensive).

Primary/Secondary Qualities: Primary qualities are inherent (solidity, extension, figure, motion, number) — our ideas resemble them. Secondary qualities (color, sound, taste) are powers produced in us — our ideas don’t resemble anything in the object.

Validity and Limits: Knowledge is perception of agreement/disagreement of ideas. Three degrees: Intuitive (direct, self-evident — circle is not triangle), Demonstrative (via proofs — certain but less clear), Sensitive (ideas of external objects — beyond doubt but not as certain). Knowledge cannot reach further than ideas. Where ideas are wanting, there can be no knowledge. Only knowledge of universal self-evident truths truly satisfies.

Q10. Discuss the existentialist conception of freedom and authenticity in Kierkegaard, Heidegger, and Sartre. (7–15 marks)

Existentialism is a philosophy against extreme rationalism, giving prominence to man’s passionate nature — anguish, guilt, freedom. Existence precedes essence: there is no fixed human nature. Human life is a series of critical situations and resolutions.

Kierkegaard: The father of existentialism, gave the term “Existenz.” Three conceptions: Truth (subjective, within the individual), Choice (expounded in Either/Or — esthetic vs ethical life), and God (the religious stage). Three stages of existence: Aesthetic (pleasure → boredom/despair), Ethical (duty/commitment), Religious (leap of faith, the “knight of faith,” Abraham’s sacrifice). Transitions require a leap, not rational progression. Choices are irrevocable. No reasoning can help — every premise presupposes another. “What is important is not what we must know but what we must do.” Boredom, anxiety, and despair accompany existence.

Heidegger: Central question: What is Being? Dasein (“being-there”) = human existence as being-in-the-world. Influenced by Brentano’s intentionality. Dasein ist sorge — Dasein is Care. Two modes of encountering things: Zuhandenheit (readiness-to-hand, engaged use) and Vorhandenheit (present-at-hand, detached observation). Dasein has the structure of thrownness (we didn’t choose our birth), projection (into possibilities), and fallenness (absorption in Das Man / the “They”). Inauthenticity = living as “one” does, conformity. Authenticity = confronting our mortality (being-toward-death). Angst reveals nothingness and is the gateway to authenticity.

Sartre: Atheistic existentialism. God is a contradictory idea. “Existence precedes essence” — man is an undetermined project. “Men are condemned to be free.” Consciousness is always consciousness OF something (intentionality). Bad faith (mauvaise foi) = denying your freedom (“I had no choice,” the waiter playing his role too perfectly). Being-in-itself (en-soi) = things, fixed. Being-for-itself (pour-soi) = consciousness, free, always becoming. Anguish (total responsibility), Forlornness (no God, no values given), Despair (act without certainty). Ethical responsibility: when you choose, you choose for all humanity.

The Ethical Self: No pre-given rules. Values are created through choices. Authenticity is the highest value. Bad faith is the fundamental moral failure. “Not to choose is to choose not to choose.” Existentialist ethics is an ethics of commitment and engagement.
